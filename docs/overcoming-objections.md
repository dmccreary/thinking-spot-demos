# Overcoming Rima's Objections

Rima already stocks and successfully sells generic "Science Kits" in the
$50–$100 range. Any pitch for custom STEM kits has to answer, directly, why
this is additive rather than a redundant, riskier version of something
already working. Below are the objections she's likely to raise, in the
order she's likely to raise them.

## 1. "I already sell science kits in this price range. Why do I need different ones?"

Her current kits are general-purpose science kits (chemistry sets, crystal-growing,
volcano models) — consumable, single-use experiments. Custom STEM kits are
**reusable electronics platforms** paired with an intelligent textbook: a kid
builds, reprograms, and rebuilds the same hardware dozens of times. They sit
next to her existing kits as a distinct category, not a competing SKU — the
pitch should open with a side-by-side comparison on the shelf, not a
replacement.

## 2. "Will this cannibalize sales of the science kits I already have working?"

Different buyer intent: a science-kit buyer wants a one-time weekend
experiment; a STEM-kit buyer wants an ongoing hobby, a club supply, or a
gift that keeps giving. Cross-merchandising (place them near, not in place
of, each other) turns this into a bigger combined basket rather than a
split one. Track both categories' sales for the first 90 days to confirm.

## 3. "What's my margin, really — after I account for support time?"

Unlike a shrink-wrapped science kit, an electronics kit can generate
customer questions and troubleshooting. This is a real, ongoing labor cost.
Answer it by pricing support into the plan up front: pair the kit with the
intelligent textbook (which answers most "why isn't this working" questions
without a staff member), and start with a small SKU count so the support
burden is boundable before scaling.

Margin is the same formula she already uses for her $50–$100 science kits:

\[
\text{Margin \%} = \frac{\text{Price} - \text{Cost}}{\text{Price}} \times 100
\]

For example, a starter kit priced at $75 with a $45 landed cost — parts,
packaging, and an amortized share of curriculum development — clears
\( \frac{75 - 45}{75} \times 100 = 40\% \), before support time. Model support
time as a per-unit labor cost added to the $45, the same way she'd account
for any labor-intensive product, and re-run the same formula before setting
the shelf price.

## 4. "What if a kit doesn't work, or a part is defective?"

Electronics have a different failure mode than a chemistry set — a bad
sensor, a bad cable, a bricked microcontroller. Needs a clear return/exchange
policy stated before launch, and a vendor relationship that will replace
defective units without a fight. This should be settled with suppliers
*before* the first kit hits the shelf, not after the first return.

## 5. "My customers are used to a $50–$100 price point. Will these kits fit that, or will I need a new price tier?"

A well-scoped starter kit (one sensor, one microcontroller, the companion
lesson) can land in the same $50–$100 range she already prices to,
minimizing sticker shock. Higher-end kits or bundles can be a later,
optional upsell once the entry tier is proven — don't open with the
expensive SKU.

## 6. "Will I need to hire or train staff who understand electronics?"

This is the real long-term cost, not the inventory. Mitigate it in the pilot:
pick kits simple enough that the intelligent textbook — not the staff member
— carries the explanation, and lean on the vendor/curriculum team for a
short staff walkthrough before the kits go on the shelf. Staff expertise can
grow over time; it doesn't need to exist on day one.

## 7. "What if the technology is out of date in two years?"

Reasonable, since electronics move faster than a crystal-growing kit ever
will. Answer: choose kits built on standard buses (I2C, I2S) and widely
supported parts rather than a single vendor's proprietary platform, so
kits can be refreshed by swapping a component, not by re-buying an entire
new product line.

## 8. "Do I have the shelf space and display fixtures for this?"

Science kits are boxes on a shelf; STEM kits benefit from a working demo
(lights, motion, sound) to sell themselves. That needs a small footprint —
one end-cap or a counter display, not a full aisle — for the pilot. This
should be scoped as "how much space for one end-cap," not "how do I
reorganize the store."

## 9. "Is there really customer demand for this, or is this a solution looking for a problem?"

The existing science-kit sales are proof people already come to her for
hands-on learning products — the STEM kits are a bet that some of those same
customers (and the teachers/homeschoolers she doesn't yet reach) want a more
advanced, reusable version. The honest answer is: start small, watch actual
sell-through against the existing science-kit baseline, and let data decide
before any larger commitment.

## 10. "What if the kids find electronics harder than a science-kit experiment and get frustrated instead of engaged?"

A poorly scoped kit can absolutely produce this outcome. The intelligent
textbook exists specifically to prevent it — a simulation before the
physical build, and small, achievable steps rather than one big project.
The pilot should test this directly: watch a few real kids/teachers use the
kit in-store before it goes to the general shelf.

---

## The Underlying Ask

None of these objections argue against trying custom STEM kits — they argue
for **starting small, next to what already works, and proving it with real
sales data before expanding.** That's the same "start with one end-cap"
pilot recommended in [Why Custom STEM Kits](why-custom-stem-kits.md) — this
page is the answer key for the questions Rima will ask before agreeing to
that pilot.
